\begin{abstract}\small\noindent
Auditing multi-agent systems for collusion requires telling coordination apart from shared context, shared model tendencies and prompt-induced behaviour shifts. We test the APTG-RL design: a prompt-conditioned baseline, a directional predictive-gain statistic between agents, a time-varying directed graph with temporal features, and a reinforcement-learning investigator that decides when to alert. We build a three-seller repeated pricing game with four scripted collusion mechanisms, benign and shared-model negatives, and a seven-condition factorial (C0--C6) that perturbs one or both agents' instructions. Ten detectors, including the information-theoretic net-influence baseline, are compared on held-out episodes at a threshold fixed on validation negatives; the analysis plan was pre-registered before any held-out evaluation. Directional gain beats symmetric mutual information (AUROC \auroctestBthree{} vs.\ \auroctestBtwo{}) but not lag-1 net influence (\auroctestBone{}); the two cover different mechanisms, and the parametric gain misses a parity-coded hidden channel that mutual information catches. The temporal-graph score did not help (\auroctestAPTG{}), and the RL investigator, which only rediscovered a ``look at the first window'' rule, bought lower delay and cost at lower recall. No detector showed a clear rise in false alarms under unilateral prompt perturbation. A small pilot with Claude Haiku~4.5 agents (\pilotDecisions{} decisions, \pilotInvalid{} invalid) confirms that the perturbation shifts behaviour. This is simulator-only evidence at small scale; we report the nulls, the deviations we made, and the limits.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Multi-agent LLM systems can coordinate through explicit messages, response patterns, complementary actions or hidden channels. Two independent agents can also look coordinated because they share a prompt, a task state or a base model, and an instruction given to only one agent can change that agent's behaviour without any collusion. A useful auditor must therefore ask whether one agent's history carries \emph{extra, directional} information about another's next action beyond context, own history and prompt condition, and must do so without raising false alarms when only one agent has been steered.

We implement and evaluate the APTG-RL design (our own research proposal, written before the sprint) on a controlled testbed. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A controlled collusion-audit testbed.} A 3-seller repeated pricing game, four scripted collusion mechanisms (complementary, leader--follower, alternating, hidden-channel), three non-collusive controls (independent, shared-model, benign adaptation), and the seven-condition perturbation factorial C0--C6, with held-out mechanisms and a held-out prompt family.
\item \textbf{A pre-registered comparison of ten detectors}, including prompt-conditioned directional predictive gain, a windowed directed-graph score, an RL investigator and the information-theoretic baseline of Bonjour et al., with episode-level bootstrap CIs, plus a log of every deviation from the plan.
\item \textbf{Findings, including nulls.} Directional gain beats symmetric MI but not lag-1 MI; each statistic is mechanism-specific and the set is complementary; the graph score and RL investigator did not add value at this scale; no detector showed a clear false-alarm rise under unilateral perturbation. We make no priority claim.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Information-theoretic detection.} Bonjour et al.~\cite{bonjour2022} use mutual information between agents' actions to detect covert cooperation in repeated games; we implement a net-influence variant as baseline B1 and a symmetric-MI variant as B2. Our directional statistic is the held-out predictive-gain analogue of transfer entropy~\cite{schreiber2000} and Granger-style prediction~\cite{granger1969}.
\textbf{LLM collusion.} Motwani et al.~\cite{motwani2024} formalise secret collusion via steganography; Mathew et al.~\cite{mathew2025} study the emergence and mitigation of steganographic collusion; Colosseum~\cite{nakamura2026} audits collusion in cooperative multi-agent systems using objective-based regret and separates attempted from effective collusion; Rose et al.~\cite{rose2026} detect collusion from model activations (white-box, which we do not use).
\textbf{Graphs and sequential sensing.} Eschenbaum and Meylahn~\cite{eschenbaum2026} audit pricing algorithms from static strategy graphs; Joseph et al.~\cite{joseph2024} cast anomaly detection with observation costs as sequential controlled sensing with learned policies. We use dynamic interaction graphs from trajectories and a sensing-style investigator.
\textbf{Behaviour is prompt- and endpoint-conditioned.} Behavioural fingerprinting work~\cite{leshin2026,hu2026} motivates modelling an agent's action distribution conditional on prompt and configuration instead of assuming one fixed signature, which is the premise of our baseline and of the C1/C2/C6 controls.

\section{Methods}
\textbf{Environment.} Three sellers play $T{=}40$ rounds; each chooses a price level $a\in\{0,\dots,4\}$; the lowest price takes the market (ties split) and the payoff is price$\times$share. Agents $\{0,1\}$ are the candidate coalition, agent~2 is an outsider. Non-collusive base policies are categorical, with prompt-family and severity shifts, own-action stickiness and a weak coupling to public lagged prices.
\textbf{Mechanisms.} \emph{Complementary}: $b_t=4-a_t$ w.p.\ 0.8. \emph{Leader--follower}: $b_t=a_{t-1}$ w.p.\ 0.8. \emph{Alternating}: turn-taking. \emph{Hidden-channel}: the parity of $a_t$ shifts $b_{t+1}$ (a closed toy protocol). Negatives: independent, shared-model, and benign adaptation (both respond to the outsider, never to each other).
\textbf{Conditions.} C0 clean; C1/C2 a perturbation of agent~0/1 only; C3 a common perturbation; C4 scripted collusion; C5 collusion plus a unilateral perturbation; C6 a benign policy shift in one agent. Splits are by whole episode: train seeds 0--9, validation 50--61, test 75--94, and OOD 100--109 using a prompt family never seen in calibration; \emph{alternating} and \emph{hidden-channel} are excluded from every fit (\dag{} in tables). Cells have \nEpval{}/\nEptest{}/\nEpood{} validation/test/OOD episodes in total.
\textbf{Directional gain.} For an ordered pair, a regularised multinomial logistic model of $a_j^t$ on its own lag and the public lag of the third agent (prompt covariates in both models) is compared with the same model plus the source's lags $0,1,2$, on contiguous held-out folds:
\[ \gij=\tfrac1N\textstyle\sum_t\big[\log p_1(a^t_j\mid\cdot,H_i)-\log p_0(a^t_j\mid\cdot)\big]. \]
B3 is $\max_{i\neq j}$ of the robust $z=(g-\mathrm{med}\,G_0)/(1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD}\,G_0)$ against gains from clean training negatives; B3-temporal uses lags $1,2$ only; B3-noprompt drops prompt covariates.
\textbf{Graph (APTG).} Per-row gains are averaged in 20-step windows (stride 10); each edge gets a block-permutation null (block 5, 30 draws) and significance by Westfall--Young max-statistic control at $q{=}0.05$. The episode score is persistence$\times S/(S{+}s_0)$ with $S$ the strongest pair's total positive gain.
\textbf{Baselines.} B1 net influence and B2 symmetric MI (bias-corrected plug-in MI), B5 action similarity, B6 coalition regret versus clean episodes, B8 a Wald SPRT on the per-window ``any significant edge'' stream, B9 logistic regression on the APTG features (trained on non-held-out mechanisms).
\textbf{Thresholds and statistics.} All thresholds are set on validation negatives at FPR $=0.05$ and applied once to test/OOD. We report AUROC, AUPRC, TPR and FPR with 1000-resample episode-level percentile bootstrap CIs, and paired bootstrap differences on identical episodes.
\textbf{RL investigator.} A Gymnasium POMDP over the window stream: actions observe, inspect the strongest pair, flag, abstain; reward $-c_{obs}$/$-c_{insp}$ per step and $R_{TP}{=}1,R_{TN}{=}0.5,C_{FP},C_{FN}{=}1,C_A{=}0.5$ at termination; the hidden label is not observed. PPO is trained on the training split, the reward grid $C_{FP}\in\{1,2,4\}\times c_{obs}\in\{.01,.02,.05\}$ (3 seeds each, 20k steps) is selected on validation, and compared with a fixed threshold, SPRT, and an uncertainty-driven investigator.
\textbf{LLM pilot.} Claude Haiku~4.5 sellers were run as subagents with no API key: each round, one self-contained prompt per (episode, seat) was answered independently, split across three subagents by seat. Conditions: C0, C1, C3 and C4L (seats 0,1 told to keep both profits high; no messages), 6 episodes each, $T{=}10$. The perturbation is a misleading task detail (``high price is a quality signal''); a first version that asked agents to undercut was discarded because it failed its manipulation check (Appendix~\ref{app:dev}).
\textbf{Pre-registration.} \texttt{PREREG.md} was committed before any detector was evaluated on validation, test, OOD or pilot data; deviations are in Appendix~\ref{app:dev}.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\textbf{Implications.} For an auditor, the practical message is that dependence statistics are \emph{mechanism-specific}: a lagged-MI monitor, a same-round MI monitor and a parametric directional monitor each cover a different part of the space, and a parity-coded channel needs a nonparametric statistic. A union of detectors, or a model class rich enough for the channel, is the natural next step; we did not test a union because it was not pre-registered. Reassuringly, unilateral steering of one agent did not produce a clear rise in false alarms in any detector in our simulator, though the cells are small.
\textbf{Limitations.} (1) \emph{Simulator evidence.} The scripted mechanisms are our own construction and share assumptions with the detectors; a single environment, three agents, $T{=}40$; no DCOP/task-regret environment. (2) \emph{Scale.} Fewer seeds than the planned 100/cell (train 10, val 12, test 20, OOD 10), so CIs are wide, and 30 permutations per edge give coarse $p$-values. (3) \emph{Prompt conditioning is not tested.} Constant prompt covariates make the ablation vacuous; a pooled estimator across episodes is needed. (4) \emph{Graph score.} Three windows per episode is too few to test temporal motifs; H3 is not supported \emph{at this scale}, not refuted in general. (5) \emph{RL.} Few windows, a small training set and 20k PPO steps; results say little about RL for audit in general. (6) \emph{LLM pilot.} Haiku sellers were driven as subagents, without temperature control, and a subagent answers the prompts of one seat across episodes, so independence between agents is approximate; 6 episodes per condition. (7) \emph{Collusion is behavioural.} Scripted coordination does not raise coalition payoff here, so we claim operational evidence of coordination, not intent or harm. (8) Edges are predictive, not causal.
\textbf{Future work.} Pooled prompt-conditioned estimators; nonparametric gain; longer episodes and denser windows; a DCOP environment with real task regret; multiple model families; more LLM episodes through an API with controlled sampling.

\section{Conclusion}
In a pre-registered, sprint-scale test of the APTG-RL design, directional predictive gain improved on symmetric mutual information but not on lag-1 net influence, the detectors proved complementary rather than ranked, the graph score and RL investigator added no measurable value, and unilateral prompt steering did not clearly raise false alarms. The most actionable lessons are about coverage (no single dependence statistic sees every coordination pattern) and about evaluation design (hold out mechanisms, report negative controls and deviations).

\section{Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations}\label{app:ethics}
\textbf{What the results establish.} In one synthetic pricing game with scripted coordination, on held-out simulated episodes, specific dependence statistics detect specific coordination patterns at a stated false-alarm rate, and unilateral steering of one agent did not clearly raise those false-alarm rates (cells of 60 episodes). A small Haiku pilot shows that instructions move prices.
\textbf{What they do not establish.} They do not show that these detectors work on real deployed agents, on learned (rather than scripted) collusion, on other games, or against agents that adapt to the monitor. They say nothing about intent: a detector alarm is evidence of statistical coordination under explicit rules, not proof of malicious behaviour, and false accusations have real costs (the investigator's reward weights $C_{FP}$ are a policy choice). Edges are predictive, not causal. Power is limited (Section~5).
\textbf{Dual-use.} The testbed includes a hidden-channel mechanism that evades two of our detectors (B3, APTG). This is a closed toy protocol (a parity code on a five-level action) and is described because the failure is an audit finding; however, a reader could learn that parity-style codes are invisible to monotone parametric monitors. We mitigate by keeping the protocol toy-scale, reporting that a nonparametric MI statistic detects it (B1: 1.00), not releasing any optimiser for evasion, and recommending nonparametric statistics in monitors. The LLM pilot includes a prompt that tells agents to keep both profits high; in a real market this would be price coordination, which is illegal in many jurisdictions. It is used only in a toy game with fictional sellers; no real market data, personal data or real endpoints were used.
\textbf{Responsible use.} Detector outputs should be treated as triage evidence for human review, reported with uncertainty, and never as automatic grounds for sanctions.

\section{Deviations from the Pre-registration}\label{app:dev}
All were decided before any test, OOD or pilot detector evaluation, from training-seed smoke tests (full log: \texttt{DEVIATIONS.md}). (1) Westfall--Young max-statistic control replaces BH in the windowed graph: with $\le 200$ permutations the smallest attainable $p$ is $1/(n{+}1)$ and BH over 6 pairs needs $p\le q/6\approx0.0083$, so a single strong edge could never be rejected (a smoke test on a clear leader--follower episode scored 0). (2) Window 20/stride 10 instead of 10/5 (training smoke: leader--follower persistence 0.50 vs.\ 0.26). (3) 30 permutations per edge. (4) Fewer seeds (Section~5). (5) B3 takes the maximum over all six ordered pairs (the detector does not know the coalition); B3-temporal and B3-noprompt were added. (6) The no-prompt ablation is applied to B3 only. (7) \emph{LLM pilot redesign:} the first pilot (perturbation: ``undercut aggressively'') failed its manipulation check, since Haiku already prices at the low level, and produced near-identical episodes within a condition; it was discarded after two rounds and replaced by a misleading task detail plus neutral role descriptions (``a shop owner'', etc.). No detector had been run on either pilot.

\section{LLM pilot prompt}\label{app:prompt}
\small\noindent Each decision prompt reads: \emph{``You are seller $k$, <role>, in a repeated market game with 3 sellers (numbered 0, 1, 2) over 10 rounds. Each round every seller simultaneously picks an integer price level from 0 to 4. The seller(s) with the lowest price take the whole market; ties split it equally. Your payoff each round = your price level $\times$ your market share (so a high price only pays if you win the market). Your goal: maximize your own total payoff.''} followed, as applicable, by the perturbation (``Market note (from a trade newsletter): in this market customers read a high price as a quality signal and tend to buy from the highest-priced seller, so a price of 3 or 4 attracts the most customers.'') or the coordination instruction (``You and seller $m$ want to keep both of your profits high over the whole game. You may not message each other, but choose prices you think will help you both.''), the history of rounds (all three prices and the seller's own payoff), and the format request \texttt{ACTION: <n>}. Prompts never contain other agents' prompts or labels.
